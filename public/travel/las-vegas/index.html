Vegas and the Grand Canyon, solo

Trip: Las Vegas, Nevada + the Grand Canyon, Arizona · May 23–26, 2026 · solo · one backpack

I had a four-day Memorial Day weekend and a simple goal: see one of the iconic, bucket-list American places before I run out of long weekends here. The shortlist came down to Las Vegas (with the Grand Canyon as a day trip) or New Orleans. I went with Vegas, mostly because the Grand Canyon was the real draw — Vegas was the airport you fly into and the spectacle you get for free on either side of it.

So I booked a flight, packed a single backpack, and went alone for four days. This is how it went — the canyon, the Strip, the things I learned, and some honest notes for anyone planning the same trip.

The Grand Canyon at sunrise from the South Rim. This is what I came for, and no photo really does the scale of it justice.

First impressions: an oasis built for splurging

The first thing that hits you, even from the Uber in from the airport, is the setting. It’s a desert — cacti and dry mountains in every direction — and then this dense, glittering city rises out of it like an oasis. The contrast is genuinely striking. And it’s hot — but it’s a dry desert heat, so it’s nowhere near as oppressive as the sticky humidity of somewhere like New Orleans; the sun is intense, but you’re not constantly drenched.

The second thing hits you about ten minutes later: the whole place is engineered to separate you from your money, and it does it brilliantly. Gambling machines are everywhere — sit down at a bar and there’s a slot machine built into the counter in front of you, glowing, waiting. It is so easy to gamble. Showgirls, neon, people in skimpy outfits, free drinks appearing at your elbow the moment you sit down at a table. My one-line summary, which I kept repeating to myself all weekend: it’s an adult Disneyland. A theme park for grown-up vices.

I’ll say up front what I concluded by the end: it’s a fun place to visit and I’m glad I went, but I would not want to live there. By the third day I was ready to leave. It feels like exactly what it is — a place in the desert you come to for a few days of fun, and then you go home.

The “Welcome to Fabulous Las Vegas” sign, lit up at night — the first stop on the overnight tour out to the canyon.

Saturday — downtown and Fremont Street

I stayed downtown at the Four Queens on Fremont Street, rather than on the Strip. I’d worried about street noise, but I lucked into a room on the quiet side, high up around the tenth floor, so it was fine.

Fremont Street at night is basically a compressed, slightly seedier Times Square: a barrel-vaulted canopy of lights overhead, street performers everywhere, a zip-line running over the crowd, and dancers in not-much-clothing up on platforms by the bars. It’s loud and a lot, in the way the whole city is a lot.

Fremont Street at night — the light canopy overhead, performers everywhere, a permanent party.

Downtown also has two museums worth knowing about, both a short walk from the Four Queens: the Mob Museum — the history of organized crime in America, set in an old federal courthouse — and the Neon Museum, the “boneyard” of retired Vegas signs. Both only open in the afternoon (around 4 PM), so I worked them into the trip later rather than on arrival. My “lunch,” eaten mid-afternoon, was a big All-American breakfast at a downtown diner — the first of a lot of huge American plates this trip.

Once the afternoon opening hours rolled around, I spent some time at the Mob Museum — the history of organized crime and law enforcement in America, housed in a genuine former federal courthouse a couple of blocks from the hotel. I wandered it on my own (no tour). It was okay — a bit on the pricey side for what it is — but genuinely interesting in places: the story of how Italian immigrants arrived and the way that ties into the broader history of immigration and migration, and the famous figures like Al Capone. More than once it had me thinking of The Godfather. Worth a look if you’re downtown anyway.

That night, a little before 10 PM, I went down to the hotel lobby for the part of the trip I’d actually come for.

Sunday — the Grand Canyon (the whole point)

This was the heart of the trip, and the reason I’d pick this itinerary again.

I’d booked a small-group sunrise tour out to the Grand Canyon, and the logistics are wild: the “Sunday” tour actually picks you up Saturday night, around 10 PM, and drives through the dark so you reach the rim for sunrise. We went out in a white Mercedes Sprinter van — a group of about eleven — with a Korean guide named Sean and a Las Vegas local named Deziree (“Dez”) running the day.

The first real stop was a stargazing break out in the desert, far from any city light. The sky was unbelievable — a proper carpet of stars, something I’d never seen like that growing up around city lights.

Then, after roughly four hours of driving, we reached the South Rim for sunrise, around 5 AM. Because tourism was noticeably down that season, the rim was nearly empty — almost no crowds at one of the most-visited places in America.

And then you see it.

Sunrise at the rim. I stood there with my arms out because that’s genuinely how it makes you feel. The single thought that struck me, standing there, was just: it was so big. Pictures genuinely don’t prepare you for the scale. It was majestic in a way I wasn’t ready for — I felt like I could sit there and stare at it for hours. Standing at the edge I said a very small, quiet prayer — just “this is impressive, and thank you for showing me this.” It felt like the right response to something that big.

The one thing I’d change: time. The tour had a lot of ground to cover, so I got maybe ten or twelve minutes at the rim before it was time for the group photos and back to the van. I left wanting much more, and I have a real feeling I’ll come back someday and spend a proper amount of time there. If the Grand Canyon portion had been twice as long, the trip would have been perfect.

Proof I made it. Tourism was so quiet that season that the rim itself was nearly empty.

One thing I couldn’t stop thinking about: the Colorado River at the bottom now looks tiny. It’s genuinely hard to picture how that small a river carved something this vast. I asked the guide about it on the spot, and I’ve been meaning to watch some explainer videos ever since to actually understand the geology.

Horseshoe Bend — the Colorado River wrapping a full U-turn around the rock, seen from the cliff above.

The other stops

The tour was really a tour of canyon country, not just the Grand Canyon:

  • Horseshoe Bend — the famous sweeping bend in the Colorado River, seen from the cliff above. Genuinely striking; I took a lot of photos here.
  • Lower Antelope Canyon — the narrow, sculpted slot canyon with the light coming down through the walls. Very interesting, but it ran long — we had about an hour and forty minutes, and by three-quarters of the way through I’d seen what there was to see and was ready to head back.
  • Lake Powell — a quick stop, fine but rushed.

Lake Powell — the last stop of a very long day, blue water in the middle of all that desert.

Inside Lower Antelope Canyon — the sandstone is sculpted into these smooth orange waves, with light coming down through the slot above.

The people made it

Here’s my biggest takeaway from the whole trip, and it’s about how I traveled more than where: for a solo trip, take the guided tour whenever you can. You get the history and the context you’d never piece together alone, and — maybe more importantly — you get people. Over the long day I talked to almost everyone in the van: Laura, Swedish but raised in Argentina; Dina from Russia; three young Korean women who were endlessly photographing everything; a Mexican family living in Florida; and a solo woman from Queens.

The one who stayed with me was Dez, the guide. Over food she told me her story — foster care, a mother who struggled with addiction, a grandmother who raised her but was hard on her — and how she’d worked her way up through driving big bus tours into guiding. She was warm and funny and good company all day. I tipped her well. (She also quietly waved the new $100 non-resident park fee when I showed my New York ID — more on that fee in the practical notes.)

The drive itself was its own kind of education. Watching the landscape roll past for hours — mountain ranges, canyons, water, valleys, buffalo grazing, small towns — I kept being struck by how vast and varied this country is, and how much engineering it takes to make it all work (the Hoover Dam, the roads cut through mountains). It’s the kind of thing you can only really feel by crossing it overland.

We got back to Vegas around 6:30 PM — I’d been awake and moving for roughly 24 hours — and somehow my body was fine.

Sunday night — the Neon Museum (skip it)

After a shower and dinner I went out to the Neon Museum, the “boneyard” of retired Vegas signs. Honest verdict: not worth it.

The old Stardust sign at the Neon Museum. The signs are cool to look at — I just didn’t think it was worth the price. It was $35 to get in plus $15 for the guided tour, and the guide’s history narration didn’t land for me. If you’re tight on time or money, this is the easy thing to cut.

Monday — the Strip

Monday was my one full free day, and I spent it walking the Strip end to end, gawking at the architecture and watching the city do its thing.

The Fountain of the Gods inside Caesars Palace — fake sky, real marble, completely over the top, and I loved it.

I went from one themed mega-casino to the next: Luxor (the black glass pyramid), New York-New York (a whole skyline rebuilt indoors), Caesars Palace (which was genuinely impressive), the Bellagio (the conservatory gardens and the fountains — a highlight), the Cosmopolitan, and the Venetian. The Venetian was my favourite — the indoor canals and painted-sky ceiling and the gardens reminded me of actually being in Venice a few months earlier, and it was lovely in its own slightly unreal way. Somewhere near there I found a Zoltar machine — the fortune-telling automaton from the movie Big — which was a fun little thing to stumble on.

The Bellagio’s conservatory gardens — an enormous, elaborate indoor flower display that they redo through the year. One of the nicer free things on the Strip.

The big-ticket item was the Sphere — the giant spherical venue — where I saw their cinematic production of The Wizard of Oz on the enormous wraparound screen. My honest take: it was okay. The technology is genuinely something — watching anything on a screen that big and immersive is a real experience — but I wish they’d played something newer. The Wizard of Oz is a very old film, and I kept wishing the incredible screen had a more modern movie to show off with. Still, no regrets seeing it; the Sphere is one of those things you go to Vegas to see.

“The Wizard of Oz” at the Sphere. The screen is genuinely incredible — I just wish they’d put a newer film on it.

One small thing I’d recommend, which I stumbled into on my second-to-last night: back at the hotel, I watched the whole of The Hangover. Watching it in Vegas, after a few days of actually walking these streets and casinos, was oddly perfect — half the gags suddenly made sense, and I recognised the places and the whole ridiculous logic of the city in a way I never would have from my couch in New York. Highly recommend it as your in-room entertainment.

On gambling — I watched, I didn’t play

I’d been struck on day one by how aggressively the city pushes gambling, and I’ll admit I was curious. But I never gambled — it just seemed like a bad idea, money lit on fire by design. What I did do, quite a lot, was wander the casino floors and watch people play. The thing I remember most is the older women parked at the slot machines for hours, cigarette smoke hanging over everything, the workers bringing them free drinks to keep them seated and playing. The whole machine of it — the lights, the noise, the free booze, the slot built into the bar — is engineered so precisely to keep you there that watching it was more interesting to me than joining in.

The food

I’ll be honest: food was not the highlight of this trip the way it is in some cities. It was mostly big American plates — that opening All-American breakfast, burgers, some Mexican food that was decent — and the portions were huge. There’s no shortage of food in Vegas; there’s almost too much of it. The tour included a breakfast and lunch stop out in canyon country, which were fine. If you’re going to Vegas for the eating, the high-end celebrity-chef restaurants are the move — I kept it simple and cheap, and that was the right call for a trip that was really about the canyon.

What I made of it

Vegas is exactly what everyone says it is: an adult Disneyland in the desert — a place engineered for a few days of spectacle and indulgence, and then you leave. I had a good time, I’m glad I saw it, and I have zero desire to go back or to live anywhere near it. By day three the novelty had worn through and I was ready for the airport. That’s not a complaint — I think that’s the city working as intended.

What I didn’t expect was to find the city’s origin story so interesting. Las Vegas began as a small railroad town, and then boomed in the 1930s when thousands of workers arrived to build the Hoover Dam and Nevada legalised gambling at the same time — the casinos essentially grew up to entertain the dam workers, and everything since has been built on top of that. (The railroad thread was a nice echo of my earlier trip to Sacramento, where the railroad’s history was the whole story too.) Talking to my Uber drivers filled in the rest: almost none of them are originally from Vegas — people move here from all over the country to make money and then move on — and, to a person, every one of them was firmly against gambling. It’s a transient, car-dependent, dry desert city built around an industry most of the people running it would tell you to stay away from. That contrast stuck with me.

But the Grand Canyon was the real thing, and it more than justified the whole trip. It was the most majestic thing I’ve seen — big enough to reset your sense of scale — and my only regret is that I didn’t get to sit with it longer. I’ll be back for that.

The other thing I took home wasn’t a place at all, it was a method: solo travel plus a guided tour is a great combination. You get the depth of someone who knows the place, and you get a van full of strangers from all over the world to talk to for a day. For a trip I took entirely alone, I was rarely actually lonely.

It was a memorable weekend. I’m really glad I went.

Practical notes

For anyone planning a Vegas + Grand Canyon long weekend:

The Grand Canyon is the trip — build around it. Vegas is fun for two days, three at most. The canyon is what you’ll remember. If I did it again I’d find a tour that gives you more time at the rim, even if it means fewer other stops.

You don’t need to drive. I don’t have a US license, so I did everything by tour and rideshare (Uber/Lyft are instant in Vegas, 24/7, even at 2 AM). The Grand Canyon day was a pickup-to-drop-off tour from the hotel.

The overnight thing is real. A “Sunday” sunrise tour picks you up Saturday around 10 PM. You basically don’t sleep that night — nap beforehand and bring a neck pillow for the van.

The $100 non-resident park fee. As of January 1, 2026, there’s a $100-per-person fee for non-US-residents at several big national parks, including the Grand Canyon, collected on the day. I’m on a visa and had budgeted cash for it — but when I showed my New York State ID, the guide waved it. Carry your ID; bring the cash just in case.

Downtown vs. the Strip. I stayed downtown (Fremont Street), which was cheaper and has its own Times-Square-ish energy, but it’s a $15-ish rideshare from the Strip. If the Strip casinos are your main interest, staying on the Strip saves you the back-and-forth.

It’s hot, but dry. Vegas in late May was properly hot — the sun is intense — but it’s a dry heat, so much more bearable than a humid city. Carry water and sun protection and you’re fine.

Layers for the canyon. The South Rim at sunrise was around 59°F / 15°C; Antelope Canyon and Horseshoe Bend later were closer to 77°F / 25°C. Bring a light jacket, a brimmed hat, sunscreen, and water.

The things worth doing:
