> **Trip:** New Orleans, Louisiana · June 19–21, 2026 · solo · one backpack

New Orleans had been on my list for a while. It's one of those American places that feels like its own country — French, Spanish, Caribbean and Southern all layered on top of each other — and I wanted to see it before I run out of weekends in the US. So I booked a flight, packed a single backpack, and went alone for three days.

This is how it went, what I ate, and the things I learned along the way — including a museum that I went in expecting to tick off a box and came out of genuinely moved.

<img src="/images/new-orleans/french-quarter-street.jpg" width=700 alt="A white mule pulling a Royal Carriages tour buggy near Jackson Square in the New Orleans French Quarter, under a bright blue sky">

*A mule-drawn carriage near Jackson Square — the first thing that hits you about New Orleans is that it doesn't feel like the rest of America.*

## First impressions: a wall of humidity

I almost missed my flight (a story for another day), but I made it, and by early afternoon I was walking from my hotel toward the French Quarter. The first thing New Orleans gives you in June is **the humidity**. I was sweating within minutes of stepping outside — it was somewhere around a 110°F "feels-like" with a heat advisory in effect. The first thing I bought in the city was a **straw hat**.

The Quarter itself is wonderful to walk through. It reminded me, oddly, a little of Bangalore at first, and then it settled into something more Caribbean — colour everywhere, wrought-iron balconies, people out eating and laughing and putting on a show. It's a place that wears its character openly.

I stayed in the **Arts / Warehouse District**, a short walk from the Quarter and, as it happened, right near the museum that would become the highlight of the trip.

## Friday — the French Quarter

I'd booked a **walking history tour of the French Quarter** for the afternoon. Honest take: it was a bit long (about two and a quarter hours) and very heavy on theory — and I made the rookie mistake of trying to power through on nothing but protein bars, so by the end I was fading. But the history stuck, and it's the kind of history that reframes the whole city:

- New Orleans passed through three hands — **French, then Spanish, then American** (after a treaty) — and you can read that in the architecture. The buildings look French, but many are actually built in the Spanish style that went up after the great fires.
- **Sicilian Italians** came later, for work.
- The land was reclaimed **swampland**, made valuable by all the sediment the **Mississippi River** dumps here. At one point this was one of the richest places in the country.
- And the fact I keep repeating to people: the city is **not named after Orléans in France** — it's named after the **Duke of Orléans**, the regent for the boy-king Louis XV. (Napoleon turns up in the story too.)

<img src="/images/new-orleans/st-louis-cathedral.png" width=700 alt="St. Louis Cathedral framed by palm trees, with the equestrian statue of Andrew Jackson in front, seen from Jackson Square in the New Orleans French Quarter">

*St. Louis Cathedral, framed by Jackson Square and the Andrew Jackson statue. It was closed by the time my tour finished on Friday — I'd have to come back for it.*

For lunch (well, late lunch) I ate at **Napoleon House**, a roughly 200-year-old landmark, where I had Creole **jambalaya** and tried their famous **muffuletta**. More on the food below — there's a lot of it.

### Preservation Hall

The evening was the surprise of the day. I went to a set at **Preservation Hall** — $28 for a standing ticket, about 45 minutes — with basically no idea what to expect. I'm not someone who knows jazz. But it was *wonderful*. At one point I closed my eyes and just listened to the instruments chime in one by one: a big upright bass, a keyboard, and three wind instruments — clarinet, trombone, and one more — carrying the melody between them. Totally worth it.

<img src="/images/new-orleans/preservation-hall-exterior.jpg" width=700 alt="The weathered exterior of Preservation Hall in the French Quarter, a crowd gathered outside under a mural reading 'KEEP YOUR HEAD UP'">

*Outside Preservation Hall before the set — an unassuming, beautifully worn old building.*

<img src="/images/new-orleans/preservation-hall.png" width=700 alt="A packed crowd inside the dim, weathered room at Preservation Hall, New Orleans, facing the band area with paintings on the worn walls">

*A packed house at Preservation Hall — standing room only, in a beautifully worn old room. I went in not knowing what to feel and came out a believer.*

Afterwards I walked **Bourbon Street** in a light rain — costumes, street performers, a kind of permanent carnival energy that reminded me a little of Times Square. I capped the night with **gumbo and a chicory coffee**.

## Saturday morning — into the swamp

Saturday I went out on a **swamp and bayou boat tour** — a covered pontoon boat (not an airboat), with a hotel pickup around 8 AM, out to the **Barataria / Jean Lafitte** wetlands south of the city. We saw **alligators**, glided through the bayou, and the guide turned out to be a great source of facts that I've been hoarding ever since:

- A **bayou** is a slow-moving body of water whose **water can flow in both directions** — unlike a normal river or canal, which always runs from high ground to low.
- **Spanish moss** is one of the great misnomers: it's **not a moss, not a parasite, and not from Spain**. It's a silvery plant that, during the **Great Depression**, was harvested as a cheap substitute for cotton to stuff cushions and pillows — and gave a lot of people work.
- **Cypress trees** here can grow as large and as useful as redwoods.
- **Marsh vs. swamp**: a swamp is forested; a marsh is treeless, floating vegetation.
- On the wildlife: everything we saw was **alligators, not crocodiles**. Alligators are relatively lazy, docile, nocturnal predators — they mostly take prey smaller than themselves and only go after a human by mistake (a squatting person or a kid slapping the water can read as "small prey"). Their teeth don't even touch when their mouths close. Crocodiles are the aggressive ones.
- A genuinely odd fact: **Louisiana — and New Orleans especially — is one of the only places you can privately own a body of water.** You can't feed gators in public waters, but in private waters the rules are looser, which is why the guides can lure them with marshmallows.

## Saturday afternoon — the National WWII Museum

This was the best thing I did all weekend, and the thing I'd most recommend to anyone visiting New Orleans.

I'll be honest about why it landed so hard. One of my long-standing weaknesses is **history and geography** — I'd pick up scattered facts in small museums and never had a frame to hang them on. The National WWII Museum gave me that frame. I did the **guided tour** plus their signature cinematic film, **"Beyond All Boundaries,"** narrated by Tom Hanks. It's not cheap (around $76 all-in), but it's worth every dollar.

<img src="/images/new-orleans/wwii-museum.jpg" width=700 alt="An exhibit hall inside the National WWII Museum in New Orleans">

*The National WWII Museum. I came in expecting to tick a box and left wanting to learn for months.*

For the first time, the whole war clicked into a **timeline** instead of a pile of disconnected facts. A few things that crystallised:

- The war "ends" in 1945, but the trouble really starts in **1919, with the Treaty of Versailles**. After that comes the Great Depression, and roughly a decade later the war proper.
- Two forces expanding at the same time — **Nazi Germany** in Europe and **Japan** in the Pacific — eventually threatening both Europe and America, until **Pearl Harbor** pulled the US fully in. (I'll admit it: before this trip I didn't even know where Pearl Harbor *was*.)
- What stayed with me most was the sheer **resilience and coordination** — building weapons and ammunition at scale, organising across an entire society without any of the communication tools we take for granted, and a country that was deeply divided by race and class at home pulling together for the war effort. It left me with a real respect for what was achieved.

It also left me with a pile of questions I'm still chasing — what was Italy's stake in joining the Axis? What was Russia's role, and why did Hitler march on Moscow? I walked out wanting to learn the **map of the world** properly and to memorise a few anchor dates so the next thing I read has somewhere to attach. The single biggest realisation: most of my confusion about history came simply from **not knowing where countries are** and **not having a timeline**. Fix those two and everything else has a place to hang.

And there was a nice little connection on the way out: the **Spanish moss** I'd learned about that very morning became popular during the **Great Depression** — which I now knew sat *before* the Second World War. Two facts from two completely different tours, suddenly linked. That's the feeling I travel for.

### Dinner at Cochon

I had dinner at **Cochon**, chef Donald Link's Cajun restaurant near the museum. It was a bit of a splurge (around $42 with tip) and worth it — proper **South Louisiana cuisine**: **fried alligator**, **wood-fired oysters**, and the namesake **cochon** — slow-cooked pork with cracklins.

## Sunday — a slow goodbye

My last morning was deliberately unhurried. I left the hotel late, wandered back into the French Quarter, and finally got **inside St. Louis Cathedral** — this time for the **11 AM Mass**, which felt like the right way to close the loop after finding it shut on Friday.

<img src="/images/new-orleans/cathedral-interior.jpg" width=700 alt="The ornate interior of St. Louis Cathedral during Mass — a vaulted, painted ceiling, chandeliers, flags hanging in the nave, and a black-and-white checkerboard marble floor">

*Inside St. Louis Cathedral during Sunday Mass. The flags down the nave represent the nations whose flags once flew over New Orleans — France, Spain, Britain and the US among them.*

Then the part of the day I'd most been looking forward to: **breakfast at Café du Monde**. I'd actually tried to go on Friday and been defeated by not carrying cash (a recurring theme — more on that below), but the main Jackson Square stand runs basically around the clock, so I came back for it. **Beignets and a café au lait** — powdered sugar everywhere, exactly as advertised. I wandered the **French Market** alongside it.

<img src="/images/new-orleans/cafe-du-monde-beignets.jpg" width=700 alt="A plate of beignets dusted with powdered sugar and a café au lait at Café du Monde, New Orleans">

*Café du Monde beignets and a café au lait. Bring cash.*

To get out to the **Garden District**, I took the **St. Charles Avenue streetcar** — one of the oldest continuously operating streetcars in the world, an olive-green wooden car that rattles down a track shaded by enormous oaks. It's a lovely, slow way to watch the city change from the dense Quarter to wide avenues and grand houses. Out there I joined a **pay-what-you-wish walking tour** of the Garden District (I caught it about halfway through, gave $15), had a mocktail at a café, and then headed for the airport.

<img src="/images/new-orleans/st-charles-streetcar.png" width=700 alt="The historic olive-green St. Charles Avenue streetcar, car number 460, crossing an intersection in downtown New Orleans">

*The St. Charles streetcar out to the Garden District — my favourite way to move around the city.*

<img src="/images/new-orleans/garden-district.jpg" width=700 alt="A huge spreading live oak arching over the corner of First Street in the Garden District of New Orleans, grand houses on either side under a blue sky">

*The Garden District — enormous live oaks arching over quiet streets of grand old houses. A completely different New Orleans from the French Quarter.*

## Music everywhere

If there's one thing that defines New Orleans more than the food or the history, it's the music. It isn't confined to venues — it's the city's baseline hum. Beyond Preservation Hall, live jazz pours out of the bars all down **Bourbon Street** and **Royal Street**, and street musicians set up on corners with a trumpet, a clarinet, a sousaphone and a bucket for tips. You genuinely can't walk far without running into a band. It's the most musical place I've ever been.

## A city built by immigrants

Down by the river I came across the **Monument to the Immigrant** — a marble statue of a family stepping off the boat, dedicated in 1995 by the Italian American Marching Club. It stuck with me more than I expected.

<img src="/images/new-orleans/immigrant-monument.png" width=700 alt="The Monument to the Immigrant in New Orleans — a white marble statue of an immigrant family on a pedestal beside the Mississippi River">

*The Monument to the Immigrant, on the bank of the Mississippi.*

So much of what's interesting about America was built by people who came from somewhere else. Here it was the **French and the Spanish**, and then the **Sicilian Italians** who arrived later for work. Standing in front of that monument, I realised it rhymed with two places I'd visited recently back in New York — the **[Museum at Eldridge Street](https://www.eldridgestreet.org/)**, a beautifully restored synagogue that tells the story of Jewish immigration, and the **Tenement Museum** on the Lower East Side, where Italian and Jewish families were packed into a single building. Finding the same story of arrival here, at the other end of the country, drove home how much of America is really just that: a story of people who left somewhere and showed up to build something new.

## The food

New Orleans food deserves its own section. Over three days I worked my way through most of the classics:

- **Jambalaya** — a rice dish with meat cooked in; mine was Creole-style at Napoleon House. Hearty, a little sweet.
- **Muffuletta** — the famous New Orleans sandwich, stacked with cured meats, cheese and olive salad. Napoleon House is one of the landmark spots for it.
- **Gumbo** — a thick stew over rice; I had a bowl with a chicory coffee.
- **Fried alligator** — yes, really, at Cochon. Tastes a bit like firm chicken.
- **Wood-fired oysters** — also at Cochon, smoky and rich.
- **Cochon** — the slow-cooked pork dish the restaurant is named after, with cracklins.
- **Beignets and café au lait** at Café du Monde — the sweet finale.
- **Chicory coffee** — the local style, coffee cut with roasted chicory root; worth trying at least once.

<img src="/images/new-orleans/nola-food.jpg" width=700 alt="Three scoops of New Orleans jambalaya — rice cooked with meat — served with crusty bread and a pat of butter">

*Jambalaya, served as three rice scoops with bread and butter. There's a reason people come to New Orleans just to eat.*

## What I made of it

Honestly, it was a good trip and I'm glad I went. It was humid, and I walked a *lot* — but walking is the way to see the place. The city is walkable enough, and the public transport (the streetcars especially) is good; the only time I needed an Uber the whole weekend was to and from the airport.

The people were what I remember most. Every guide I had — the French Quarter history walk, the Garden District tour, the swamp tour — was warm and genuinely good company. That's not a given, and it coloured the whole trip.

One small surprise: it wasn't cheap. I'd assumed a Southern city would be a fair bit cheaper than New York, and it really wasn't — not by much. But the things worth doing were worth the money. If I were advising someone, I'd say **skip the big riverboat cruise and do the swamp tour instead**, and **spend an evening at Preservation Hall for the jazz** — those were the right calls. And just sitting by the water for a while was lovely in its own quiet way.

The best thing, by some distance, was the **National WWII Museum**. I came away having genuinely learned — about the war, and about the French and Spanish layers of the city's own history. That's exactly what I want from travel: to come home knowing more than I did, and to have it stick.

It's been a memorable trip. I'm really glad I went.

## Practical notes

For anyone planning their own New Orleans weekend:

**When to go.** Avoid high summer if you can — June was brutally humid with heat advisories. Spring or autumn would be far kinder.

**Where I stayed.** The **Arts / Warehouse District** — walkable to both the French Quarter and the WWII Museum, and quieter than staying right on Bourbon Street.

**Carry cash.** I lost a beignet run and couldn't tip a couple of times because I only had cards. Lots of the small, good experiences here run smoother with cash on hand.

**Eat real meals.** The food is half the reason to come — don't try to power through the day on snacks like I did.

**The things worth booking:**

| Thing | Notes | Rough cost |
|---|---|---|
| **National WWII Museum** | The highlight. Get the **"Beyond All Boundaries"** film and consider the guided tour. Give yourself more time than I did. | ~$76 all-in |
| **French Quarter history walk** | A licensed-guide history tour (I did **Friends of the Cabildo**, from Jackson Square). History-dense — eat first. | ~$30 |
| **Swamp / bayou tour** | Covered pontoon boat with hotel pickup, out to the Barataria / Jean Lafitte wetlands. | ~$75 with pickup |
| **Preservation Hall** | Standing-room jazz, ~45 min. Punches way above its ticket price. | $28 standing |

**Getting around.** The French Quarter is very walkable. The **St. Charles Avenue streetcar** is both transport and an attraction — take it out to the **Garden District**.

**Don't miss:** Café du Monde beignets (the Jackson Square stand runs ~24h), St. Louis Cathedral on Jackson Square, and a Garden District walk.
